# Excursion X3 — The education / learning-science field for keeping a working expert current (findings)

**Question:** Beyond the classroom-assessment mechanisms already surveyed in X1 (BKT, spaced repetition, mastery learning, self-explanation), what does the education / learning-science field offer for the specific problem of *keeping a capable practitioner current on concepts as they arise in real work* — and what transfers to a single-human, text-agent setting where agents write to one expert "architect"?

**Method:** ~9 web sources, primary/academic where available. Research only — no solution designed. Each section states what transfers vs. what does NOT for a single **expert** learner, as a scoped null. Does NOT re-cover X1's four mechanisms.

---

## 1. Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) — the four-component architecture

**Mechanism.** The canonical ITS is built from four interacting components ([ScienceDirect overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/computer-science/intelligent-tutoring-system); [Comprehensive AI-ITS review, arXiv 2507.18882](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.18882v1); [Padayachee, *ITS: Architecture and Characteristics*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228921731_Intelligent_tutoring_systems_Architecture_and_characteristics)): (a) the **domain model** (a.k.a. expert/knowledge model) — the subject matter itself: facts, concepts, rules, problem-solving strategies, and the "expert" reference against which learner state is judged; (b) the **student (learner) model** — the learner's current knowledge, misconceptions, and preferences, and the *basis from which the system adapts*; (c) the **tutoring (pedagogical) model** — decides *when and how to intervene, what to present, how to deliver it*, using pedagogical rules; and (d) the **interface**. The loop: the domain model informs the tutor; the student model personalizes the tutor's decisions; the interface communicates. A recurring design point in the literature is that the student model is usually expressed *as an overlay on the domain model* — the learner's knowledge is represented as "which parts of the expert domain map does this person currently hold," rather than as a free-standing structure.

**Transfers.** The four-way decomposition maps almost directly onto the design at hand: the **"concept book" is the student model** (what the architect currently holds), a codebase/architecture map is the **domain model** (the concepts that exist and how they relate — see §5), and the **"tutor agent" is the tutoring model** (deciding when a concept is worth surfacing and at what register). The most transferable structural insight is the *overlay* idea: model the human's knowledge as a lightweight annotation over an existing domain map, not as a separately-authored corpus. This is cheap and keeps the two in sync.

**Does NOT transfer.** Classic ITS student models are populated by **instrumented performance data** — the system watches the learner solve constrained problems inside its own interface and infers knowledge from correct/incorrect steps (this is exactly the graded-response barrier X1 identified). Our architect works in the real codebase, not inside a tutor's problem interface, and emits prose, not solved problem-steps. So the *architecture transfers but its data pipeline does not*: the student model must be fed from spontaneous writing and work-context signals, not from monitored problem-solving. NOT evaluated: model-tracing / constraint-based tutor internals, which need a formal domain expert model our setting lacks.

## 2. Scaffolding & fading; the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)

**Mechanism.** Vygotsky's ZPD is the band between what a learner can do alone and what they can do *with help from a more capable other* — the productive target is just inside that band ([SimplyPsychology: ZPD](https://www.simplypsychology.org/zone-of-proximal-development.html); [Cogn-IQ encyclopedia](https://www.cogn-iq.org/learn/theory/vygotsky-zone-proximal-development/)). Scaffolding operationalizes teaching in that band via three features: **contingent** support (the *smallest* help that re-enables a productive attempt), **fading** (gradual withdrawal as competence grows), and **transfer of responsibility**. The key operational concept is **contingency**, often called the single most important property of scaffolding: the tutor continuously monitors and *calibrates support up or down* — Wood/Bruner's "contingent instruction" rule is that after a failure you give a *more specific* directive immediately, and after a success you offer a *less* explicit one next time. That two-directional rule is precisely how "just above current ability" is estimated (probe, then step support up on failure / down on success) and how fading decides to stop explaining a now-known concept (repeated success → withdraw). A 2025 systematic review of 158 studies notes AI tools are increasingly built to *estimate a learner's ZPD and supply contingent, fading support* as "digital scaffolders" ([arXiv 2512.05506](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05506)).

**Transfers.** The **contingency rule as a fading heuristic** transfers cleanly and is arguably the field's best answer to "when do I stop explaining a concept the human already knows": if the human uses a concept fluently / correctly in their own writing, step the explanation *down* (or drop it to a bare mention); if they stumble or ask, step it *up*. This is a graded register dial, not a binary explain/don't. It also frames "just above ability" correctly: surface concepts *at the edge* of what the architect already commands, not deep foundations they own and not far-off material with no hook.

**Does NOT transfer.** ZPD/scaffolding assumes a **tight probe-response loop with a more-capable tutor** who can inject a task and read the immediate reaction. In a text-agent setting the agent is *not* more capable than the architect in the domain (inversion — see §3), and there is no reliable turn-by-turn probe; the only "response" is whatever the human happens to write back. So contingency must run on *sparse, asynchronous, self-generated* signal rather than on elicited task performance. Fading works; the calibrating probe that classically drives it does not exist and must be substituted by passive reading of the human's own usage.

## 3. The expertise-reversal effect & the expert blind spot — THE CRITICAL DIFFERENCE (expert vs novice)

**Mechanism — expertise reversal.** This is the field's strongest, most directly relevant result. Grounded in cognitive load theory, the **expertise reversal effect** (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler, Sweller) is a *reversal in the relative effectiveness of instructional methods as learner knowledge rises*: techniques that strongly help novices lose effectiveness and can become *actively harmful* for experts ([Kalyuga et al. 2003, *The Expertise Reversal Effect*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4419-8126-4_12); [Kalyuga 2007 PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)). It is a special case of the **redundancy effect**: a novice lacks domain schemas and needs external guidance to avoid overloading working memory; an expert already holds the schema internally, so the *same* guidance is now redundant information the expert must effortfully cross-check against what they already know — *raising* cognitive load and depressing performance. Concretely, support that helps novices but **hurts experts**: worked-out step-by-step examples, integrated/elaborated textual explanations embedded in diagrams, redundant restatements, and heavy instructional guidance. For experts the reverse wins: eliminate redundant representations and worked-out steps, prefer reduced guidance, open-ended problems over worked examples, and "study" replaced by minimal pointer-level prompts. The prescribed remedy is **guidance fading** — strip scaffolding as expertise grows.

**Mechanism — expert blind spot.** The complementary hazard sits on the *teacher* side. The **expert blind spot** (Nathan, Koedinger & Alibali 2001; Nathan & Petrosino 2003) is "the inability to perceive the difficulties that novices will experience," because experts have automated the intermediate steps and *forget what is non-obvious* ([Nathan & Koedinger, *When Content Knowledge Eclipses Pedagogical Content Knowledge* PDF](https://website.education.wisc.edu/mnathan/Publications_files/2001_NathanEtAl_ICCS_EBS.pdf); [Nathan & Petrosino 2003, AERJ](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00028312040004905)). Notably, the *most* domain-expert teachers were the *most* likely to mispredict novice difficulty, and experts consistently *overestimate* how well an explanation landed.

**Transfers (this is the core finding).** Our learner is an **expert-in-domain wanting peer register**, so the entire novice toolkit is contraindicated by expertise reversal. What the field says to do for an expert: (1) **omit the scaffolding** — no worked steps, no from-first-principles build-ups, no restating what they own; redundant explanation is not neutral, it *degrades* the exchange and reads as condescension (the register complaint). (2) Teach at **pointer/peer altitude** — name the concept, the delta from what they already know, the load-bearing consequence, and stop; assume the schema and connect to it rather than constructing it. (3) **Fade aggressively and by default** — the expert's baseline is "minimal," and you add depth only on a detected miss, which is the inverse of the novice default. The expert-blind-spot result adds a second, subtler warning that cuts the *other* way and is easy to miss: an agent that is itself "expert" in the concept will *under-explain the genuinely novel piece* — glossing the one non-obvious step because it feels obvious to the explainer. So for an expert learner the correct register is "peer, minimal, assume-the-schema," but with deliberate care to still surface the *actually-new* delta rather than assuming the expert already has it.

**Does NOT transfer.** The experimental fading curves are calibrated on *measured* expertise (test scores gating each fading step); we have no such measurement, so "how expert, on this specific concept, right now" is inferred, not known — and expertise is *concept-local* (the architect may be expert on module A and genuinely novice on a newly-introduced concept B). The transfer is the **direction and default** (start minimal, add on miss), not a validated schedule. NOT evaluated: whether register mis-calibration in text (too-low vs too-high) has the same performance cost as the visual/worked-example manipulations these studies used.

## 4. Just-in-time / situated learning & cognitive apprenticeship

**Mechanism.** Situated cognition (Brown, Collins & Duguid 1989) holds that knowledge is *in part a product of the activity, context and culture in which it is used* — learning is most durable when it happens in an authentic context resembling where the knowledge will be applied, rather than as decontextualized curriculum ([Brown, Collins & Duguid, *Situated Cognition and the Culture of Learning* PDF](https://www.johnseelybrown.com/Situated%20Cognition%20and%20the%20culture%20of%20learning.pdf); [Wikipedia: Cognitive apprenticeship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_apprenticeship)). **Cognitive apprenticeship** (Collins & Brown) is the instructional model built on it, structured as *modeling → coaching → scaffolding → fading*, in which a master makes normally-invisible expert reasoning *visible* while the apprentice works on real tasks, and coaching is delivered "at the skill level just beyond what the learner could accomplish alone" (ZPD again). The unifying idea for our problem: teach the concept **at the moment of need, embedded in the real work**, not ahead of time as a syllabus.

**Transfers.** This is the strongest *situational* match and directly validates the "teach the concept when it comes up in the build" premise. Two things transfer: (1) **just-in-time surfacing** — a concept introduced *because it just became load-bearing in the current work* is exactly the authentic-context condition the theory says maximizes retention and transfer; the agent already sits inside the real work, so this comes almost for free. (2) **Making expert reasoning visible** — the apprenticeship value isn't the fact, it's exposing the *why/when-to-use* around it; for an expert peer, the transferable slice is surfacing the non-obvious rationale or tradeoff at the point it bites, not the definition.

**Does NOT transfer.** Cognitive apprenticeship assumes a *master → apprentice* asymmetry (the agent models expert practice for a less-skilled learner). Our relationship is closer to *peer-to-peer or apprentice-to-master* in the domain — the human is often the more expert party — so "modeling expert reasoning" inverts and can misfire as the expert-blind-spot condescension of §3. The apprenticeship *sequence* (heavy modeling first) does not transfer; the *just-in-time, in-context* delivery principle does. NOT evaluated: the social/legitimate-peripheral-participation strand (Lave & Wenger), which assumes a community of practice rather than a single human.

## 5. Concept maps / prerequisite (knowledge) graphs — dependency-ordered teaching

**Mechanism.** Concept maps (Novak, Cornell, 1960s, grounded in Ausubel's subsumption theory that new learning attaches to relevant *prior* knowledge) represent knowledge as **nodes (concepts) + labeled links (relations)**, surfacing the *sequencing of topics and their prerequisite dependence* ([IHMC: *Theory Underlying Concept Maps*](https://cmap.ihmc.us/docs/theory-of-concept-maps); [Novak concept-mapping guide, MSU](https://cf.psl.msu.edu/ctools/novak.html)). The modern, computational form is the **prerequisite knowledge graph**: a directed graph where an edge A→B means "A is prerequisite to B," used to generate dependency-ordered learning paths ("learn basic concepts before complex ones"). Recent work automates *constructing* these graphs and inferring prerequisite edges — e.g. AI-assisted educational knowledge-graph construction with prerequisite relations ([ACE, *Journal of Educational Data Mining*](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/737); [*Exploring knowledge graphs for concept prerequisites*, Smart Learning Environments](https://slejournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40561-019-0104-3)).

**Transfers.** A **concept dependency graph is the natural shape for the domain model** in §1, and it answers a real design question: *at what depth do I explain concept B?* — gate depth on whether the architect already holds B's prerequisites. If they own the prerequisites, a peer-register pointer suffices; if a prerequisite is missing, that's where a (brief) bridge is warranted. It also gives principled **ordering**: when several new concepts land at once, surface them in dependency order so each rests on already-established ground. This reinforces X1's "ordering not gating" conclusion from mastery learning.

**Does NOT transfer.** The graph is used in classic settings to *sequence a whole curriculum* and to *block* advancement until prerequisites are mastered — neither applies: the architect is not walking a curriculum and cannot be gated (they jump anywhere in the real codebase). Also, auto-*constructing* an accurate prerequisite graph is a nontrivial research problem requiring a corpus; for a single codebase the dependency structure may be better read off the architecture map than learned statistically. So the *representation and depth-gating heuristic* transfer; the *path-planning / prerequisite-gating* usage does not. NOT evaluated: the graph-neural-network prerequisite-inference methods (need training data our single-human setting lacks).

## Bottom line for a single **expert** learner served by text agents

- **The dominant, must-obey finding is expertise reversal (§3):** for an expert wanting peer register, the *entire* novice support toolkit — worked steps, from-scratch build-ups, redundant restatement — is not merely wasted but *harmful* (raises load, reads as condescension). Default to **minimal/pointer register; add depth only on a detected miss** (inverse of the novice default). Counter-balance with the expert-blind-spot warning: still surface the *genuinely-new delta* rather than assuming the expert already holds it, and note that expertise is **concept-local** (expert on A, novice on new B).
- **The ITS four-component architecture (§1) is the clean structural skeleton:** concept book = student model (as an *overlay* on a domain map), architecture map = domain model, tutor agent = tutoring model. Borrow the decomposition; drop its instrumented-performance data pipeline.
- **Fading is the answer to "stop explaining a known concept" (§2):** the contingency rule — step register *down* on fluent use, *up* on a stumble — is a graded dial driven by the human's own writing, not by an elicited probe (which doesn't exist here).
- **Just-in-time, in-context delivery (§4) validates the core premise** — teach the concept when it becomes load-bearing in the real build — but the master→apprentice asymmetry inverts (the human is often the expert), so surface *rationale/tradeoff at point of need*, not modeled expert practice.
- **A concept dependency graph (§5) gives depth-gating and ordering:** explain B at peer-pointer depth when its prerequisites are held, bridge only a missing prerequisite; order simultaneous concepts by dependency. Representation transfers; curriculum sequencing and prerequisite *gating* do not.
- **Recurring mismatch (stated once):** every mechanism here was built for a *novice in a tutor's problem interface producing gradeable responses*. Ours is an *expert in real work producing prose*. The transferable core across all five: model familiarity **per concept** on a domain graph, default to **minimal expert register**, and **fade contingently on the human's own spontaneous usage** — surfacing concepts **just-in-time** as they become load-bearing.
