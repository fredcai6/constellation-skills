# R6 — ERS Deployment Inference from Speed/Throttle/Gear Traces Alone

Question: what is honestly inferable about F1 energy/ERS deployment from speed, throttle, and gear traces ALONE (no ERS/SOC telemetry channels), per the optimal-control/energy-management literature — including the 2026 regulation change (50/50 split, MGU-K power increase, deployment/derating along straights, X-mode/Z-mode)?

Method: web search + fetch across three lanes — (a) academic optimal-control/energy-management literature for F1/hybrid race cars, (b) a genuinely on-point 2026 arXiv preprint that inverts exactly this problem (opponent energy-state inference from public telemetry), and (c) grey-literature/practitioner methodology (a Substack that builds and runs a real trace-only deployment classifier on 2026 pre-season data). No peer-reviewed paper was found that inverts a bare speed/throttle/gear trace to recover ERS state as its primary contribution — the closest thing is (b), a single-author, not-yet-peer-reviewed preprint, flagged accordingly throughout. Treat this excursion's numeric claims as bounded by that gap.

---

## 1. What deployment/harvest/derate signatures look like in a speed/accel trace

**Theoretical starting point — bang-bang control.** The foundational literature (Limebeer et al., *Optimal control of Formula One car energy recovery systems*, Int. J. Control 2014) frames ERS deployment as a Pontryagin minimum-principle problem: costs and dynamics linear in the control (deploy/don't-deploy) push the optimal solution to **bang-bang switching** — full power or none, not a graduated ramp. In principle this predicts a genuinely detectable feature: a step-like discontinuity in longitudinal acceleration at the moment MGU-K assist switches on or off, superimposed on the smooth aero-drag-only deceleration/acceleration curve a naturally-aspirated, non-hybrid baseline would produce. [Limebeer 2014 abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00207179.2014.900705), [Limebeer presentation](https://www.citystgeorges.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/280927/Limebeer-Presentation-22-10-2014.pdf)

**Two things blunt that clean step in practice**, both confirmed by later sources:
- Real implementations relax bang-bang throttle into something continuous (PID-controlled, or an adjustable engine/motor power-split) precisely because a literal step is hard on the drivetrain and tyres — so the discontinuity becomes a *fast ramp*, not an instantaneous jump. [Limebeer 2014, via search synthesis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00207179.2014.900705)
- Under 2026 rules the MGU-K power ceiling itself tapers with speed rather than being a fixed cap (§4), so even a bang-bang *policy* decision (deploy: yes/no) produces a *curved* acceleration signature once the car is above ~290 km/h, not a step — described explicitly as "not an on-off switch... the acceleration rate starts to decrease more quickly than it would from aerodynamic drag alone," i.e. a smooth curve-over rather than a kink. [F1 Chronicle — MGU-K Rampdown Explained](https://f1chronicle.com/mgu-k-rampdown-explained/)

**Practitioner-grade operational definitions (2026, real cars, no SOC channel)** — a Substack ("The Overcut") independently built and ran a five-regime classifier against 2026 pre-season telemetry using only speed/throttle/brake/gear/position, which is the closest thing found to a working answer to this exact question:
- **Deployment** flagged where observed acceleration exceeds a car's own **25th-percentile acceleration at that speed/gear** (a conservative, per-car empirical baseline standing in for "engine-only" capability), assumed deployment rate ≈250 kW continuous.
- **Harvest** (under braking) estimated from kinetic energy lost, ΔE_k = ½m(v₁²−v₂²), capped by an assumed 0.25 MJ/s max harvest rate and an assumed 8.5 MJ/lap harvest ceiling.
- **"Super-clipping"** (drag-only/engine-derate under full throttle — i.e., the car is being held back despite full throttle, either by MGU-K rampdown or ICE derate) flagged via unexpected deceleration residual against a **drag-only baseline built from reference laps**, requiring both an absolute threshold (Δa beyond ≈0.3 m/s²) and a statistical-outlier test (z-score < −2) to suppress false positives.
- The author reports concrete, plausible-looking outputs from this method on real 2026 pre-season laps (net battery drain across a qualifying-sim lap vs stable SOC across a race-sim lap; a Red Bull "banking" pattern; Aston Martin apparently harvest-limited, inferred from extended lift-and-coast) — but this is **one enthusiast's unvalidated model against ground truth**, not a peer-reviewed method, and the piece does not claim or demonstrate accuracy against real SOC data. [The Overcut — Inside F1's 2026 Energy War](https://theovercut.substack.com/p/inside-f1s-2026-energy-war-reverse)

Net: the literature and practice agree that trace-only detection is *methodologically real* (percentile-baseline acceleration residuals, kinetic-energy harvest accounting, outlier-based derate detection) but every method found is either (a) a theoretical prediction never validated against real telemetry, or (b) a practitioner heuristic validated only informally against its own plausibility.

## 2. Typical per-lap deployment pattern (the prior)

Consistent across all sources, old and new regulations: **deploy under acceleration/full-throttle (corner exit, straights), harvest under braking.** This is the assumed, essentially unchallenged prior in every optimal-control paper reviewed. Refinements found:

- The *timing structure* of optimal deployment is not a fixed template — Balerna et al. (IEEE Trans. Vehicular Technology, 2023, "Minimum-Race-Time Energy Allocation Strategies for the Hybrid-Electric Formula 1 Power Unit") found that **the marginal lap-time value of a unit of battery deployment varies over the course of the lap**, and used a nonlinear-program allocation framework to set lap-by-lap fuel/battery *targets* for a race, precisely because naively deploying whenever available is not optimal. [IEEE Xplore 10018239](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10018239/), [ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/367194192_Minimum-Race-Time_Energy_Allocation_Strategies_for_the_Hybrid-Electric_Formula_1_Power_Unit)
- **Circuit dependence of the harvest side is strong and load-bearing**: at some circuits, rear-braking energy alone is enough to fund the lap's optimal deployment; at others there isn't enough braking, meaning the achievable *energy-neutral* deployment strategy is circuit-specific, not a single universal pattern. [same source, search synthesis]
- The 2026 preprint (§3 below) operationalizes this same idea quantitatively: circuit-dependent recharge availability ranges **1.0× (energy-scarce: Australia, Italy, Saudi Arabia) to 2.2× (regen-rich: Azerbaijan, Singapore)** relative to a nominal need, and at the low end this pattern-flips the prior — "super-clipping" (forced non-deployment) becomes the **modal, near-mandatory** state rather than a discretionary strategic signal, which degrades exactly the kind of trace-based inference discussed in §1 (everyone looks the same, so deviations stop being informative). [arXiv 2603.01290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01290)

## 3. A 2026 preprint that directly attacks this inverse problem — treat as unreviewed

**Kleisarchaki, "Opponent State Inference Under Partial Observability: An HMM-POMDP Framework for 2026 Formula 1 Energy Strategy"** (arXiv 2603.01290, submitted 1 Mar 2026, current v3 15 May 2026) is the one source found that poses exactly R6's question as its research problem: infer a rival's hidden energy state from public trace data, with **no SOC/telemetry access**, because under 2026's 50/50 split the *optimal* strategy is genuinely a function of the opponent's hidden state (a partially-observable stochastic game, not solvable by single-agent optimization).

**Caveat up front:** this is a single-author arXiv preprint with no evidence of peer review found. Numbers below are pulled from the full-text v1 (the only version whose complete text I could retrieve); the current v3 abstract reports materially better headline numbers (96.8%/89.4%/96.3% vs the v1 figures below, on an expanded 40-state/6-signal model vs v1's 30-state/5-signal model) — I could not verify v3's method section directly, only its abstract, so treat the exact numbers as version-dependent and unverified beyond what's quoted.

**Observable inputs used (v1, five signals, all *deltas from a per-driver rolling 5-lap baseline*, at 10 Hz via FastF1):**
1. Speed-trap speed delta (Δv_trap, km/h vs rival baseline)
2. Sector time delta (Δt_sector, s)
3. Braking-point delta (Δb_brake, distance from apex, m)
4. Detrended within-sector speed variance (σ²_speed)
5. Active-Aero (X-mode) binary flag inferred from straight-line speed signature (z_aero)

Notably **throttle and gear are not in this list** — the paper works from speed/sector/braking-point features derived from timing-and-speed feeds, not raw throttle traces, which is a narrower observable set than R6's premise (speed+throttle+gear+brake+DRS). That likely makes R6's actual data richer than what this paper needed to hit its reported accuracy.

**Reported accuracy (v1, synthetic race data only):** 3-level ERS accuracy 92.3% (random baseline 33.3%); full 30-state (ERS × Override × tyre-degradation joint) top-1 accuracy only 46.3% (random baseline 3.3% — so far above chance but the joint state is much harder than the marginal); Override-Mode-available-vs-spent accuracy 67.3% (random baseline 50% — barely better than a coin flip); "counter-harvest trap" detection recall 95.7% across 117 synthetic trap sectors.

**Explicit, load-bearing limitations the paper states about its own method:**
- Override Mode status (available vs already spent) is **not reliably distinguishable in the non-active state** — the two conditions differ by <0.5 km/h in the trap speed signal, below usable resolution.
- Harvest vs. derate are **not separately identifiable** — both produce the same "below-baseline" L-state signature; the paper infers which one contextually (e.g. from circuit recharge-availability priors) but does not claim to observe the distinction directly.
- Tyre degradation is only detectable at the "cliff" (sudden falloff); smooth/normal degradation is **absorbed into the same rolling baseline** used to detect ERS deployment, i.e., normal tyre wear and subtle deployment differences are confounded with each other by construction of the method.
- Fuel load is excluded from the inferred state entirely, treated as deterministic — meaning any team doing something non-standard with fuel (extra ballast, aggressive fuel-saving) would bias the residual baseline in a way the model can't separate from an energy-strategy signal.
- The model is explicitly a **synthetic-data validation only**; the paper states calibration against real 2026 telemetry "begins with the Australian Grand Prix" and that the model "should be treated as empirically validated only from Race 4 onwards" because of a ~24:1 parameter-to-data ratio after a single race (i.e. it's asserting its own numbers above are optimistic upper bounds, not real-world performance).
- Expected calibration error (ECE) of 0.11 on synthetic data is described as "a lower bound on real-data calibration error" — the belief-state probabilities the model outputs are known to be overconfident even in the easy synthetic case.

**Deliberate deception defeats simple trace-reading — the "counter-harvest trap".** The paper formally proves (its Proposition 1) that **no single-threshold rule on any one observable can reliably detect deception**: a car genuinely in high-deployment mode (H) without Active Aero produces a trap-speed delta of about +4 km/h; a car deliberately harvesting (L, −2 km/h) while running Active Aero (+4 km/h contribution) produces a nearly identical composite of about +2 km/h to a car genuinely in medium-deployment mode (M, +1 km/h) — the distributions overlap enough that scalar thresholding cannot separate a genuine intermediate state from a car that is *actively disguising* its harvest as deployment. Separating them requires joint inference over multiple observables simultaneously (in this case, the aero-mode flag plus the speed-trap delta together), and even then only reaches ~80% separation probability at the individual-observation level. This is the single most important finding for R6's null-scoping: **trace-only inference is adversarially defeatable in principle**, and the defense (multi-signal belief-state fusion) is itself only partially effective and unvalidated on real data.

## 4. What the 2026 regulation change does to all of the above

Sourced from F1.com, Mercedes-AMG F1 technical pages, and F1 Chronicle explainers (secondary/journalistic, not primary FIA text — the primary FIA 2026 PU technical regulations PDF could not be retrieved through available tooling, so treat the numbers below as reported-by-journalism, cross-checked across 2+ independent outlets each unless noted):

- **50/50 ICE/electric power split** (vs. ~80/20 under 2014–2025 regs): MGU-K max output rises from 120 kW to **350 kW**; ICE max drops to roughly **400 kW**, converging the two contributions. This is confirmed across multiple independent sources. [F1.com — 2026 PU regs explained](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/2026-regulations-explained-all-you-need-to-know-about-f1s-new-power-units.14jfv7a36905uDJDdNyfQd), [F1 Chronicle — MGU-K explainer](https://f1chronicle.com/what-is-mgu-k-f1/)
- **MGU-H removed entirely** — simplifies the powertrain but also removes a whole prior recovery/deployment channel that older analysis had to account for; 2026-era trace inference only has to reason about one motor-generator (MGU-K), not two.
- **MGU-K power rampdown curve**: full 350 kW available below **290 km/h**; power tapers on a defined (reportedly non-linear, "curve-over" rather than a kink) profile between **290–355 km/h**; **zero additional MGU-K contribution above 355 km/h**. This is a deliberate, safety-motivated regulatory choice (capping terminal velocity given X-mode drag reduction stacked with 350 kW), not an engineering limit — confirmed independently by two sources. [F1 Chronicle — MGU-K Rampdown Explained](https://f1chronicle.com/mgu-k-rampdown-explained/), [search synthesis of FIA reg language](https://f1chronicle.com/what-is-mgu-k-f1/)
- **Manual Override Mode**: a separate, proximity-gated mechanism (available to a following car within a defined gap, similar spirit to old DRS) that **extends the speed range over which full MGU-K power is available** beyond the base 290 km/h taper-start point — one source states an extended full-deployment ceiling around 337 km/h under override, and Override activation also grants a stated **+0.5 MJ bonus recharge allowance**. These override-specific numbers come from single sources each and were not independently cross-checked — flag as less certain than the base rampdown numbers above.
- **X-mode/Z-mode active aero**: driver-selected, low-drag (X, straights, flap-open, available on straights >~3s) vs high-downforce (Z, corners, flap-closed) wing states, independent of but *stackable with* Override Mode — a following car can run X-mode (less drag) and Override (extended MGU-K range) simultaneously, which is the regulation's replacement mechanism for DRS-style overtaking assistance. [F1.com — X-mode/Z-mode explainer](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/explained-2026-aerodynamic-regulations-fia-twitter-mode-z-mode-.26c1CtOzCmN3GfLMywrgb2)
- **Per-lap harvest capacity**: reported figures conflict across sources and could not be reconciled — one line of reporting states harvest capacity is **nearly doubled to ~8.5 MJ/lap** (up from ~2 MJ under the old MGU-K-only braking-harvest cap), while a Mercedes technical-specs page states the W17's "maximum useable energy storage range on track" is **4.0 MJ**. These may not be measuring the same quantity (per-lap harvest ceiling vs. usable battery capacity vs. per-lap deployment ceiling are three different numbers that 2014–2025-era regs kept separate: historically ~4 MJ/lap deployment limit, ~2 MJ/lap MGU-K harvest limit, unlimited MGU-H harvest) — this is flagged as an open reconciliation gap, not resolved in this excursion.
- **Consequence for trace-only inference**: the 50/50 split and MGU-K power near-tripling mean deployment/harvest now dominates the speed trace far more visibly than under the old ~20%-electrical regime — the Overcut piece's whole method (§1) is *only viable in 2026* because the electrical contribution is now large enough relative to total propulsive force to show up as a detectable residual against an ICE-only baseline. Under the old 120 kW/20%-split cars, the same signature would have been a much smaller fraction of total acceleration and correspondingly harder to separate from noise (aero, tow, wind, driver line variance) — no source quantified this claim directly, but it follows from the stated power numbers and is implicitly why none of the pre-2026 optimal-control papers reviewed (Limebeer 2014, Balerna 2023, Javed & Samuel 2022) frame their work as an *inference* problem at all — they're all forward-looking design/strategy optimization for a team that already has the SOC data, not trace-reading for an outside observer. The Kleisarchaki preprint (§3) is the first source found to frame this as an inverse problem, and it is explicitly a 2026-regulation-motivated paper for exactly this reason (the game only becomes adversarial/opponent-dependent once both cars' electrical contribution is large enough to matter strategically).

## 5. Magnitude — how much lap time deployment strategy is worth

- **Old-generation KERS (2009–2013, 60 kW / 6.67 s per lap, 400 kJ storage)** — the simplest, best-documented historical case: reported lap-time benefit **≈0.1–0.4 s per lap**, and deployment was visible enough in period broadcast telemetry that press analysts routinely called out the boost-button acceleration bump directly (this is old, well-established ground truth, included as a calibration anchor for "how visible does deployment need to be to matter").
- **Modern hybrid-era energy management sensitivity**: Balerna et al.'s time-optimal gearshift + energy management work found that a **4% deviation from the optimal upshift threshold costs >100 ms** — evidence that energy/gearshift strategy is a fine-grained, continuously-optimized lever, not a coarse strategic choice, which cuts against the idea that deployment differences between drivers will show up as large, easily-separable signal.
- **Qualitative but repeated claim across 2026-focused sources**: with the split moving to 50/50, ERS management is now described (by multiple journalistic/technical sources, not a controlled study) as **"the defining factor of lap time,"** i.e., the stakes of getting deployment strategy right are asserted to be much higher in 2026 than under the ~20%-electrical old regs — consistent with, but not independently quantified beyond, the power-share numbers in §4.
- No source gave a single, well-controlled, 2026-specific number for "seconds per lap separating optimal from suboptimal ERS strategy" — this is a genuine gap (Null 2 below).

## 6. What is honestly inferable, synthesized

**Reasonably inferable from a 10–20 Hz speed/throttle/brake/gear/DRS(X-mode) trace alone, with real but bounded confidence:**
- Coarse, relative deployment level (more aggressive vs. more conservative than a driver's own rolling baseline) — this is the one claim with both theoretical grounding (bang-bang/rampdown physics) and two independent implementations (Overcut's percentile-residual classifier; Kleisarchaki's HMM emissions), and the HMM paper's synthetic 3-level accuracy (92.3%, well above 33% chance) is the best quantified confidence bound found, with the caveat that it is synthetic-only and uses a narrower five-signal feature set than R6's premise.
- Phase-level structure (deploy on straights/corner exit, harvest under braking) — universally assumed and consistent with vehicle dynamics; not really "inferred" so much as a strong, essentially unchallenged prior from every source reviewed.
- Gross regime flags: whether a car is being held back at full throttle in a way inconsistent with drag alone ("super-clipping"/derate detection via outlier residuals) and whether Active Aero/X-mode is active (from the straight-line speed-vs-drag signature) — both have stated, if informal, detection criteria.
- Circuit-level priors on how constrained a car's strategy is likely to be (energy-scarce vs. regen-rich circuits), which can contextualize any trace-based read.

**NOT reliably recoverable from trace data alone, per the sources reviewed:**
- Exact battery SOC in MJ or % — every method found produces coarse bins/residuals or a probabilistic belief state, never a claimed direct reading; the HMM paper explicitly frames its output as a probability distribution over 3–4 discrete levels, not a continuous estimate.
- Instantaneous MGU-K power output or the exact ICE/MGU-K torque split at a given instant — confounded by X-mode aero state, fuel load, wind/tow, and unknown per-car engine-mode calibration (no source claims to separate these).
- Whether Override Mode is available-but-unused vs. already spent, while not actively firing — the one paper that tested this directly found it's below observable resolution (<0.5 km/h signal difference, 67.3% accuracy against a 50% baseline).
- Harvest vs. derate as distinct causes of a "below-baseline" observation — conflated in the one method that tried to separate them; only contextually disambiguable via circuit priors, not observably.
- Deliberate strategic deception (the counter-harvest trap) — formally proven undetectable by any single-threshold trace read; even multi-signal fusion tops out around 80–96% depending on validation regime, and none of that is validated against real 2026 race data as of the source's writing.
- Team-proprietary deployment calibration/maps, per-driver style differences in how they use a given energy budget, or specific engine-mode selection ("party mode" vs. conservative) — no source claims any observability into these beyond aggregate pace effects.
- Exact per-lap harvest/deployment energy ceilings under the 2026 regs — even the *regulatory* numbers are not fully reconciled across secondary sources (§4), so any inference pipeline built on an assumed ceiling (as the Overcut method does, hardcoding 8.5 MJ/lap and 0.25 MJ/s) is calibrated against a contested external number, not a verified one.

## Scoped nulls

1. **No peer-reviewed paper found that inverts a bare speed/throttle/gear trace to recover ERS state as its primary research contribution.** The one directly on-point source (Kleisarchaki, arXiv 2603.01290) is a single-author, not-yet-peer-reviewed preprint; its own text states the model is validated only on synthetic data and asks to be treated as unvalidated on real telemetry before the fourth race of 2026. Everything in §3 should be read with that ceiling.
2. **No controlled, 2026-specific quantification of "seconds per lap gained from optimal vs. suboptimal ERS deployment" was found.** §5 gives an old-KERS anchor (0.1–0.4 s) and a gearshift-sensitivity anchor (>100 ms per 4% deviation), but nothing dated to the 2026 50/50-split regime with a stated lap-time delta.
3. **The FIA's own 2026 Power Unit Technical Regulations PDF could not be retrieved through available tooling** (fetch attempts failed); every number in §4 is sourced from journalism/team-technical-page secondary reporting, cross-checked across 2+ outlets where possible but not against the primary regulatory text. The per-lap harvest-capacity figure (8.5 MJ vs. 4.0 MJ) is an explicit, unresolved conflict between two such secondary sources.
4. **No source (academic or grey-literature) reported a validated accuracy number for trace-only ERS inference against real ground-truth SOC telemetry.** The Overcut piece shows plausible-looking output on real 2026 cars but never checks it against a real SOC channel; the Kleisarchaki paper's accuracy numbers are all synthetic-data self-evaluation. If f1Brainz needs a real accuracy bound, it does not yet exist in the literature and would have to be estimated in-house against whatever ground truth (if any) is available.
5. **Whether the theoretical bang-bang discontinuity (§1) is empirically visible at all in real 21st-century F1 data was not directly tested by any source** — it's a 2014 control-theory prediction, qualified by later sources as smoothed by both real-world control implementation and the 2026 rampdown curve, but no source ran the actual test (fit a drag-only baseline to real telemetry and looked for a residual step).
